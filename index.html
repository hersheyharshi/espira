Can electrostatic forces clean up low Earth orbit?

I designed ESPIRA, a spacecraft that charges orbital debris and moves it with electric fields. Then I tested the design against the physics. It failed, in a way that points to a better answer.

By Harshita Pandita

01 — THE PROBLEM

Low Earth orbit is filling up faster than it cleans itself.

Every collision in orbit creates more fragments, and each fragment can cause another collision. ESA's annual space environment report concludes that new debris now outpaces natural re-entry, and that active removal is needed. Without it, a runaway cascade (Kessler syndrome) could make some orbits unusable.

Most removal concepts grab debris physically with nets, harpoons or robotic arms. That means matching the motion of a tumbling, uncooperative object. I wanted to know whether it could be done without touching it.

02 — THE CONCEPT

Borrow the physics of an industrial dust filter.

Factory chimneys clean smoke with electrostatic precipitators, which charge each particle and then let an electric field pull it out. ESPIRA applies the same three steps to debris in orbit.

STEP 1 · CHARGE

An electron beam or UV light deposits charge on the debris from a few metres away.

STEP 2 · FIELD

Four high-voltage plates around the spacecraft create an electric field.

STEP 3 · MOVE

The charged object is pulled in for storage or pushed toward re-entry, without contact.

03 — THE TEST

Every number in the design was a target. So I checked the physics.

I wrote a first-order model of what actually happens to a charged spacecraft in orbit. It covers how far the field reaches through the surrounding plasma, how much charge the plates hold, the resulting force, and the power needed to keep the plates at high voltage.

The key fact I had missed: low Earth orbit is not a vacuum. It sits inside the ionosphere, a plasma that pulls charge off any high-voltage surface. Try it yourself below.

04 — WHAT I FOUND

As designed, ESPIRA cannot work in low Earth orbit.

Three results settle it. Each is large enough that better engineering would not close the gap.

15×

Too little power

Holding the plates at 20 kV in LEO plasma needs about 19–24 kW. The solar array provides 1.4 kW.

6 vs 100

Too little force

Even with no plasma at all, a month of push gives a 100 kg object about 6 m/s. Lowering its orbit enough to re-enter needs about 100 m/s.

106

The web cannot stop debris

A 1 cm fragment crossing the 200 m web at 10 km/s carries a million times more energy than the field can remove. It would cut the filament.

SubsystemVerdictReason
MYCENETNot viableWith realistic debris densities, a 10 cm object would cross the web about once every 175 years. Faster fragments cut the filaments.
SPINDARDoubtfulThe magnetic signal of weakly magnetic materials fades as 1/r³, so identification at 5–15 m is not established.
ELECTROSKINWorth testingIt works at contact range, so plasma screening does not limit it. Survival in vacuum and UV is the open question.
NEUROTRIAGEMinor gainEvent-driven chips save energy, but computing is a small share of the power budget.
STIGMANot viableCharge marks on debris are neutralised by the plasma in well under a second.
05 — WHAT I'D BUILD INSTEAD

The plasma that defeats electrostatics in LEO is exactly what a tether can use.

The analysis does not end the idea. It shows where each version of it belongs.

GEOSTATIONARY ORBIT · 36,000 KM

Use electrostatics, slowly.

The plasma there is hot and thin, so the field reaches hundreds of metres and holding high voltage costs watts, not kilowatts. A servicer can tow a dead satellite to a graveyard orbit over weeks. This is the electrostatic tractor, studied since 2011 at CU Boulder, and its published forces match my estimates.

field reach ≈ 235 m  ·  power ≈ 0.2 W
LOW EARTH ORBIT · 400–800 KM

Use the plasma as a resource.

A bare conductive tether a few kilometres long collects electrons from the ionosphere. The current crossing Earth's magnetic field produces a drag force with no propellant. This is the electrodynamic tether, developed for deorbit kits in the EU-funded E.T.PACK project.

F = I·L·B ≈ 1 A × 1 km × 30 µT ≈ 30 mN (100× ESPIRA)

Two ESPIRA parts carry over: the adaptive-grip net (ELECTROSKIN) and the tumble-aware capture logic. Both could help a chaser attach a tether deorbit kit to a large object such as a spent rocket stage.

06 — WHAT I LEARNED

Test the idea before polishing it.

"The most useful result of this project was finding the assumption I hadn't questioned."

I designed five new subsystems for ESPIRA before checking whether its core mechanism worked where I meant to use it. A short calculation with standard plasma physics would have shown the problem on day one.

The bigger lesson was about the environment. I had treated space as empty, and in low Earth orbit it is not. Good space systems engineering starts from the environment and designs with it rather than against it.

07 — NEXT STEPS
  1. Reproduce a published electrostatic-tractor result in GEO, using the multi-sphere method, to validate the model.
  2. Model the deorbit time of an electrodynamic tether kit on a ~1 t object from 550–800 km, and compare it with a chemical deorbit burn.
  3. Simulate a tumbling object striking a passive net and an adaptive-grip net, to test whether ELECTROSKIN really reduces bounce-off.
